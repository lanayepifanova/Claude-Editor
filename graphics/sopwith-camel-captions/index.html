This is the Sopwith Camel.
It is one of the most famous planes
from World War I because it shot down
nearly 1,300 enemy aircraft,
which is more than any other Allied
fighter of the war.
There's a story of a Camel pilot named Roy
Brown, who was officially credited
with shooting down the Red Baron,
which is Germany's top ace.
It was a British single-seat fighter,
and the Sopwith Aviation Company built
it to replace their earlier fighter,
which called the Pup, and it reached
the Western Front in 1917.
So what made it so deadly was how tightly
it could turn.
A dogfight is won by getting behind
the enemy, and almost nothing
could out-turn a Camel.
It was also very, very dangerous to fly.
So more than 380 pilots died in accidents
while learning to fly it, which is almost
as many as died in combat.
It got its name from a hump in front
of the pilot, which covered its two
Vickers machine guns.
So the guns fired straight
through the propeller, and they were timed
so that the bullets went between
the spinning blades.
The tight turning came from its rotary
engine, which means the whole engine spins
around with the propeller.
A heavy spinning engine acts
like a gyroscope, and that let the Camel
whip around to the right faster than
almost any other fighter.
It also made the nose drop in right turns
and climb in left ones.
With the engine, guns, fuel, and pilot all
packed into the front, the plane
was twitchy and very hard to control.
So good pilots loved
it, but it also caught out
a lot of new ones.
The Camel had one more weakness,
which was that it was a biplane,
and its two wings were held together by
struts and wires that pushed
it against the air.
They held its top speed to about 185
kilometres per hour, and that made it burn
through lots of fuel.
So the Camel could turn really fast,
but it couldn't fly very fast or far.
And after the war, the next challenge
was flying long distances.
