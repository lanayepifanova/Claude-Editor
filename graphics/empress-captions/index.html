Mining royalties go back
hundreds of years,
to when anybody
who wanted to mine
the king's land had
to give the king a share
of what they dug up.
Mining companies still
make that same kind
of deal today,
and some companies
do nothing else
but collect that share.
A modern royalty company
gives a mining company
money to build or expand
a mine.
In return, it
gets a royalty,
which is a percentage
of the mine's revenue.
In 2004, the company now
called Wheaton Precious
Metals came up
with a second kind
of deal called a stream.
A stream is the right
to buy part
of the mine's gold
or silver at a fixed low
price, often around 20%
of the market price.
Either way, the mining
company pays all
the costs of running
the mine, so when gold
and silver prices rise,
the royalty company
earns more without
its costs rising too.
That is why the largest
royalty companies
have done better than
gold itself.
Since 2011, an average
of four of them,
including Franco-Nevada
and Wheaton,
has returned 597%,
compared with 286%
for gold and 160%
for a fund of gold
mining stocks.
Empress Royalty,
which trades as EMPR
on the TSX Venture
Exchange and EMPYF
in the US, is a small
company using the same
model.
Its chief executive,
Alexandra Sherron, spent
a decade at the mining
investment bank,
Endeavour Financial,
and its chairman, David
Rhodes, is one
of Endeavour's managing
directors.
At Endeavour, they kept
seeing smaller mining
companies that needed
this kind of money,
while the big royalty
companies mostly funded
large mines.
So they started Empress
to fund the smaller
ones.
The clearest example
is a silver mine
in Durango, Mexico,
called Tahuehueto,
which is run by Luca
Mining.
Empress paid US$5
million for the right
to buy all the mine's
silver at 20%
of its price, until 1.2
million ounces have been
delivered.
Luca built the mine
and pays to run it.
As of June 30, 2026,
the stream has brought
Empress US$28 million
in revenue.
In the first quarter
of 2026 alone,
it brought in about 6.4
million, more than four
times as much as a year
earlier.
Tahuehueto is one
of the four producing
investments, along
with gold streams
on mines in Peru, South
Africa, and a gold
royalty in Mozambique.
Empress put $23 million
into the four,
and as of June 30, 2026,
they had brought in 47
million.
Its yearly revenue grew
from $1.8 million
in 2022 to a record
$17.2 million in 2025,
the year it reached
positive operating
cash flow.
All four were small
deals.
In September 2026,
Empress announced a much
bigger one, $62 million
for a gold stream
on the Tongon mine
in Côte d'Ivoire.
Tongon was built by
Randgold, later run
by Barrick,
and has produced over 3
million ounces of gold
since 2010.
Under the announced
terms, Empress
would receive 3.58%
of the mine's gold
at first, and then
a smaller share for
29 years.
For each ounce,
it would pay just 0.5%
of the gold price.
The deal came
through Endeavour,
which has worked
with the seller Appian
Capital for years.
Appian is also lending
Empress $55 million
of the price, so Empress
did not have to issue
new shares,
which would have diluted
existing shareholders.
The deal still
has to close.
Management estimates
that Empress's shares
trade at about 1.0 times
its net asset value,
which
is what its royalties
and streams are worth,
minus its debt.
Its peers average 1.45
times.
That estimate assumes
gold at 4,000 US dollars
an ounce and silver
at $70, so it changes
when prices do.
The risks are real,
Empress does not run
any of these mines,
so its revenue depends
on how well
other companies run
them, and on gold
and silver prices
which can fall.
Much of its revenue
so far has come
from Tahuehueto alone.
The mines are in Mexico,
Peru, South Africa,
Mozambique, and Côte
d'Ivoire, which all
carry political
and regulatory risks.
The Appian loan adds
debt, and if cash flow
falls short, the company
may have to issue
new shares.
It is also a small
company worth about $138
million Canadian dollars
as of September 2026,
so its shares
can be thinly traded.
