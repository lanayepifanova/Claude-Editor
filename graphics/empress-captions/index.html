A modern royalty company
gives a mining company
money to build or
expand a mine.
In return, it gets
a royalty,
which is a percentage
of the mine's revenue.
In 2004, the company now
called Wheaton Precious
Metals came up
with a second kind
of deal called a stream.
A stream is the right
to buy part
of the mine's gold
or silver at a fixed low
price, often around 20%
of the market price.
Either way, the mining
company pays all
the costs of running
the mine, so when gold
and silver prices rise,
the royalty company
earns more without
its costs rising too.
That is why the largest
royalty companies
have done better than
gold itself.
Since 2011, an average
of four of them,
including Franco-Nevada
and Wheaton,
has returned
597%,
compared with 286%
for gold and 160%
for a fund of gold
mining stocks.
Empress Royalty,
which trades as EMPR
on the TSX Venture
Exchange and EMPYF
in the US, is a small
company using the same
model.
The clearest example
is a silver mine
in Durango, Mexico
called Tahuehueto,
which is run by Luca
Mining.
Empress paid US$5
million for the right
to buy all the mine's
silver at 20%
of its price, until 1.2
million ounces have been
delivered.
As of June 30, 2026,
the stream has brought
Empress US$28 million
in revenue.
Tahuehueto is one
of the four producing
investments, along
with gold streams
on mines in Peru, South
Africa and a gold
royalty in Mozambique.
Empress put US$23
million into the four,
and as of June 30, 2026,
they had brought in 47
million.
Its yearly revenue grew
from US$1.8 million
in 2022 to a record
US$17.2 million in 2025,
the year it reached
positive operating
cash flow.
All four were small
deals.
In September 2026,
Empress announced a much
bigger one, $62 million
for a gold stream
on the Tongon mine
in Côte d'Ivoire.
The deal came
through Endeavour,
which has worked
with the seller Appian
Capital for years.
Appian is also lending
Empress US$55 million
of the price, so Empress
did not have to issue
new shares,
which would have diluted
existing shareholders.
The deal still
has to close.
Management estimates
that Empress's shares
trade at about 1.0 times
its net asset value,
which
is what its royalties
and streams are worth,
minus its debt.
Its peers average 1.45
times.
That estimate assumes
gold at US$4,000
an ounce and silver
at $70, so it changes
when prices do.
The risks are real,
Empress does not run
any of these mines,
so its revenue depends
on how well
other companies run
them, and on gold
and silver prices
which can fall.
Much of its revenue
so far has come
from Tahuehueto alone.
The mines are in Mexico,
Peru, South Africa,
Mozambique, and Côte
d'Ivoire, which all
carry political
and regulatory risks.
The Appian loan adds
debt, and if cash flow
falls short, the company
may have to issue
new shares.
It is also a small
company worth about
C$138 million
as of September 2026,
so its shares
can be thinly traded.
